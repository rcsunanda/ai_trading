## **4. Why did this happen? The underlying causes**

I would separate the causes into four categories rather than attribute everything to one event.

### **4.1. The AI investment cycle is being questioned**

Most important structural factor

This is arguably the most interesting aspect of the correction.

For several years, markets have rewarded companies exposed to AI infrastructure:

- Nvidia and AMD: AI accelerators.
- Micron: high-bandwidth memory and semiconductor memory.
- Broadcom: networking and custom AI chips.
- TSMC: advanced semiconductor manufacturing.
- Microsoft, Amazon and Alphabet: cloud infrastructure and AI investment.
- Data-centre infrastructure, networking and power suppliers.

The underlying investment thesis has been straightforward:

1. AI adoption will grow substantially.
2. Companies will need enormous computing capacity.
3. Hyperscalers will spend hundreds of billions on infrastructure.
4. Semiconductor and infrastructure suppliers will capture substantial profits.
5. Those profits will justify exceptionally high valuations.

The problem is that markets are increasingly questioning the assumptions behind this thesis.

**The central question is no longer whether AI demand exists. It is whether the returns on the enormous capital investment will justify the spending.**

There are several related concerns:

- Are hyperscalers investing too aggressively?
- Will AI inference economics eventually justify the infrastructure expenditure?
- How quickly will AI infrastructure depreciate or become obsolete?
- Will competition drive down the pricing and profitability of AI services?
- Are the projected revenues sufficiently large to support the valuations being assigned to AI companies?

This distinction matters enormously.

AI can be a transformative technology, and AI companies can continue growing rapidly, while their stocks still decline substantially because their previous valuations assumed even more optimistic outcomes.

AP specifically identified concerns about the sustainability of AI spending and the scale of data-centre investment as important contributors to the sell-off.&#x20;

### **4.2. Interest rates and the Federal Reserve**

Important macroeconomic catalyst

This is arguably the most important factor outside the AI sector itself.

On 18 November, the Federal Reserve released minutes from its October meeting.

The minutes revealed that:

- Nearly all policymakers anticipated additional rate increases if the economy developed as expected.
- A vast majority were concerned about persistent inflation.
- Some policymakers believed a 50-basis-point increase might become necessary under certain inflation scenarios.

Markets increasingly priced in a December rate hike. AP reported that expectations had approached 95%.&#x20;

Why does this matter so much?

Consider the valuation of a growth company.

A company expected to generate substantial profits five or ten years from now is particularly sensitive to the discount rate used to value those future earnings.

Higher interest rates generally reduce the present value of distant cash flows.

This is especially relevant to companies with:

- High price-to-earnings ratios.
- Significant expected future growth.
- Limited current profitability relative to their valuations.
- Large capital expenditure requirements.

In other words, higher interest rates create a double challenge for expensive AI-related equities:

1. Their future earnings are discounted more heavily.
2. Their infrastructure investment becomes more expensive to finance.

This provides a plausible explanation for why technology stocks were disproportionately affected.

There is also an important contradiction worth highlighting: Treasury yields fell sharply on Friday as investors moved into bonds, even though rate-hike expectations remained elevated.

That suggests two competing forces were operating simultaneously: monetary tightening expectations and defensive positioning.

### **4.3. Valuations and crowded positioning**

This is something I consider particularly important.

The AI trade had become an extremely popular investment theme.

When investors collectively own similar assets based on similar assumptions, the market can become vulnerable to sudden reversals.

Consider the following sequence:

1. AI stocks rise substantially.
2. More investors enter the trade.
3. Valuations become increasingly dependent on optimistic assumptions.
4. Investors become less tolerant of disappointing news.
5. A negative catalyst triggers selling.
6. Falling prices trigger stop-losses, risk reductions and potentially leveraged liquidations.
7. Selling spreads into other assets as investors reduce portfolio exposure.

This can produce a much larger decline than the original fundamental news would independently justify.

## **8. My broader analysis: What does this tell us about the market?**

I think there are several deeper questions worth considering.

### **8.1. Is this an AI bubble beginning to deflate?**

This is probably the most important long-term question.

I would separate the AI investment opportunity into three different layers:

**Layer 1: AI adoption and demand**

Underlying opportunity remains

Businesses are adopting AI, model capabilities continue to improve, and demand for computing infrastructure remains substantial.

A correction in AI stocks does not invalidate these developments.

**Layer 2: AI infrastructure economics**

Increasing uncertainty

This is where I see the most legitimate fundamental questions.

Massive capital expenditure must eventually translate into adequate returns.

Investors need evidence of:

- Sustainable AI revenue growth.
- Improved infrastructure utilisation.
- Attractive returns on invested capital.
- Profitable AI products and services.

**Layer 3: AI stock valuations**

Most vulnerable

Even excellent companies can become poor investments when purchased at excessive valuations.

A company can beat earnings expectations and still experience a substantial share-price decline if investors were expecting even more.

My assessment is that the current correction is primarily a repricing of the second and third layers, rather than evidence that the first layer has collapsed.

That is a meaningful distinction when evaluating long-term investment opportunities.

### **8.2. Are we seeing the beginning of a broader economic slowdown?**

This is less certain.

There are two competing interpretations.

**Interpretation A: A valuation correction**

- AI stocks had become expensive.
- Investors became increasingly concerned about capital expenditure.
- Interest-rate expectations shifted.
- Investors reduced exposure to high-valuation companies.
- The decline spread through portfolio repositioning.

Under this scenario, the underlying economy can remain reasonably healthy.

**Interpretation B: An early warning of deteriorating economic conditions**

- Persistent inflation prevents monetary easing.
- Higher interest rates constrain borrowing and investment.
- Companies reduce capital expenditure.
- AI infrastructure spending slows.
- Corporate earnings expectations decline.
- The correction spreads into other sectors.

Under this scenario, the stock-market decline could be the beginning of something more serious.

I would not yet conclude that we are entering a recession.

However, the combination of persistent inflation, prospective monetary tightening and weakening market breadth deserves attention.

There is a potentially uncomfortable combination developing:

- Inflation remains above the Federal Reserve's target.
- Policymakers are considering additional rate increases.

This makes the environment particularly challenging for growth stocks.

It also complicates the traditional assumption that the Federal Reserve will quickly cut rates whenever equity markets experience a significant decline.

### **8.4. The AI infrastructure investment cycle could become more selective**

I think investors should pay particular attention to this.

There is a difference between companies that benefit from AI investment and companies that ultimately capture sustainable economic value from AI.

For example:

- Semiconductor suppliers benefit when infrastructure expenditure increases.
- Cloud providers benefit when customers purchase computing services.
- AI application companies benefit if they can monetise their products.
- Businesses adopting AI benefit if productivity improvements exceed their implementation costs.

These are not necessarily equivalent investment propositions.

If the industry transitions from aggressive infrastructure construction towards demonstrating profitability, the relative winners could change.

This could create opportunities in some companies while permanently reducing the growth expectations assigned to others.

## **10. Is this a buying opportunity or a warning to stay away?**

A 20% decline does not automatically make a company undervalued.

Equally, a market correction of this magnitude can create opportunities for long-term investors.

I would distinguish three situations.

Opportunity

**High-quality companies with intact fundamentals**

Companies whose earnings outlook remains strong, whose balance sheets are healthy and whose valuations have become more reasonable.

These are the companies I would investigate first.

Wait and assess

**Companies whose valuations depend on optimistic assumptions**

Particularly AI infrastructure companies where future spending, profitability and returns on capital remain uncertain.

A further correction is possible even if the underlying businesses remain excellent.

Higher risk

**Companies whose investment thesis is deteriorating**

Companies experiencing deteriorating earnings, weakening competitive positions, excessive capital requirements or financial difficulties.

Their share prices may continue declining even after the wider market stabilises.

Put/call ratios

- Index-only put/call ratio.

- Positive dealer gamma generally encourages hedging that can dampen price movements.
- Negative dealer gamma can encourage hedging that reinforces price movements.

But I would not claim that the November 20 sell-off was definitively caused by negative dealer gamma without reliable positioning data.

## **4. What I would do with trades already in progress**

This is where I would make the most practical changes. The correct response depends on whether your existing positions are long, short or still mostly in cash.

### **A. If you bought during last week's sell-off**

Suppose you followed the earlier staged-entry approach and deployed an initial 20% of your intended investment allocation.

- **I would generally hold the initial position**, assuming it was in quality companies or a diversified index and the original investment thesis remains intact.
- I would not automatically add another tranche after Monday's surge.
- Keep the remaining capital available. A pullback or a few sessions of consolidation would offer a better opportunity to assess whether the rally is sustainable.
- If a position has become disproportionately large because of Monday's rebound, consider restoring your intended position sizing rather than allowing one volatile stock to dominate your portfolio.

I would not sell a fundamentally sound long-term holding simply because the market had a volatile week.

### **B. If you entered short positions or bearish trades**

This is where I would make the most significant change.

Monday's rebound creates substantial short-squeeze risk, especially in technology and semiconductor stocks. If you opened short positions based on last week's breakdown, the original trade setup has weakened.

My approach:

- Consider closing or substantially reducing short positions that were intended as short-term momentum trades.
- Do not increase bearish exposure merely because you believe the broader market remains overvalued.
- If retaining a hedge, keep it appropriately sized and define the maximum acceptable loss.

I would be particularly careful with leveraged inverse ETFs and short-dated put options. A sharp rebound can destroy their value quickly.

### **C. If you have not entered yet**

I would not chase Monday's rally.

Instead, I would use the following decision framework:

| What happens next?                             | My response                                     |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Market consolidates and holds Monday's gains   | Begin selective entries                         |
| Market pulls back modestly, then stabilises    | Prefer this as an entry opportunity             |
| Market reverses sharply and breaks recent lows | Remain defensive; reassess                      |
| Market continues surging without a pullback    | Avoid chasing; wait for a more attractive entry |

### **D. Revised deployment strategy**

For the illustrative $10,000 tactical allocation we discussed previously, I would now adjust the plan slightly:

| Stage                 | Allocation | Condition                                              |
| --------------------- | ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Initial entry         | 20%        | Already deployed, or only on a controlled setup        |
| Confirmation          | 20%        | Market holds its recovery and breadth improves         |
| Pullback entry        | 30%        | A retracement stabilises without breaking the recovery |
| Recovery continuation | 20%        | Broader participation and sustained strength           |
| Reserve               | 10%        | Unexpected volatility or exceptional opportunity       |

These are deployment guidelines, not instructions to buy mechanically at each stage. If the market deteriorates, the uninvested capital should remain in cash.

**One important adjustment:** I would no longer make a further 5–8% market decline a prerequisite for deploying capital. If the market genuinely stabilises and earnings expectations remain sound, waiting for a much lower price could mean missing a meaningful part of the recovery.

## **5. Long-term investment thesis: what changes?**

My longer-term view is largely unchanged. Monday's rally does not materially improve the long-term investment case, just as last week's sell-off did not fundamentally destroy it.

The important distinction is between **business quality, valuation and the price you pay**.

I would retain the previous preference for quality companies with strong cash generation, defensible competitive advantages and credible earnings growth.

### **Updated view on the main candidates**

| **Berkshire Hathaway (BRK.B)** | Useful diversification and defensive exposure                           | Valuation and capital allocation                                                       |

I would particularly distinguish between Alphabet and AMD.

- **Alphabet:** I see it as a comparatively balanced investment thesis. It has substantial existing cash generation, an established advertising business, Cloud growth and the potential to monetise AI. The main question is whether AI changes the economics of Search faster than Alphabet can adapt.
- **AMD:** I see it as a more execution-dependent growth investment. Its opportunity is meaningful, but it needs to demonstrate that it can capture a durable share of the AI accelerator market and generate attractive returns from that opportunity.

I would not increase my long-term allocation to Nvidia solely because it surged on Monday. The rally was driven partly by a leadership-related event, not a fundamental reassessment of its earnings outlook.

### **My revised long-term guidelines**

1. **Continue staged accumulation.** Do not abandon the previous plan because of either last week's panic or Monday's euphoria.
2. **Prioritise valuation.** A 20% fall does not necessarily make a stock cheap. Compare its forward earnings multiple, free-cash-flow yield and growth expectations with its historical valuation and competitors.
3. **Avoid excessive AI concentration.** Owning several AI-related companies does not necessarily provide meaningful diversification if their earnings all depend on the same hyperscaler capital-expenditure cycle.
4. **Watch earnings revisions.** If analysts begin materially lowering forward earnings estimates, that would be more concerning than a temporary price decline.
5. **Retain cash flexibility.** I would rather buy a genuinely attractive company at a sensible price than force capital deployment because the market has become more exciting.

## **6. My overall recommendation as of Monday's close**

**Short-term trading**

Cautiously constructive

Monday materially improved the immediate market picture. Reduce bearish exposure, avoid chasing the rally and look for pullbacks or consolidation to establish selective long positions.

**Long-term investing**

Constructive, selective

Keep the quality-company thesis. Continue staged purchases, with particular attention to Alphabet, Microsoft and Amazon. Treat AMD as a higher-risk growth opportunity and be valuation-sensitive with Nvidia and the semiconductor sector.

**Market bottom**

Not confirmed

Monday's rebound is encouraging, but narrow leadership and unresolved macroeconomic and AI-investment concerns mean I would not yet declare the correction over.

**My central judgement:** Last week's caution was justified, and Monday is a reason to become more constructive—not a reason to become aggressive. I would rather enter a little later with better confirmation than risk committing too much capital to a one-day rebound.
